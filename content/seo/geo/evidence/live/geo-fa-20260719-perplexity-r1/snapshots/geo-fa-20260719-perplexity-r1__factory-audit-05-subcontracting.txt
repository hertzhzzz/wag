Key Subcontracting Warning Signs in a Factory Audit

When auditing a Chinese factory as an Australian buyer, the biggest subcontracting risks come from unauthorised “hidden factories” that actually make your goods while the audited site is just a showroom or admin office. Look for mismatches between claimed capacity and what you can see on the floor, plus documentation that doesn’t line up with real production.

1. Capacity vs. Capability Mismatches

These are the most direct red flags that production may be outsourced:

Reported output far exceeds visible capability

The factory claims very high monthly capacity, but you see too few machines, lines, or workers to realistically hit those numbers.
ecqa

Abnormal finished-goods spikes

Warehouse or outbound records show sudden large quantities of finished products appearing without corresponding raw materials, work-in-progress, or machine runtime to justify it.
medium

Missing or minimal in-house processes

The factory says it does everything (moulding, assembly, printing, packaging), but you only see assembly/packing; key processes appear to be done off-site.
tradeaiders

As a rule, if the factory’s story about “how your product is made” doesn’t match what you physically observe, assume some of it is subcontracted unless proven otherwise.

2. Documentation and Record Inconsistencies

Subcontracting often shows up first in the paperwork:

Production records don’t match machines or shifts

Daily/weekly production logs show output that the visible equipment and staffing couldn’t achieve in the recorded time.
medium

Raw material and component records are vague or missing

No clear traceability from raw materials to finished goods; purchase orders, inbound delivery notes, or inventory records look incomplete or generic.

Inconsistent batch numbers or product codes

Batch codes, labels, or internal job numbers don’t align with what’s on the production floor or in the warehouse; multiple “styles” of labels or packaging suggest different origins.

Quality control/QC records look templated or unrealistic

For an Australian buyer, ask for raw material invoices, inbound logistics documents, and internal transfer records; hidden subcontractors usually can’t produce a clean, consistent chain of evidence.

3. Physical and Operational Clues on Site

During the audit walk-through, pay attention to these signals:

“Demo” factory feel

The site looks overly clean, staged, or underutilised; machines are running but not at serious volume; workers seem to be “performing” rather than in normal production rhythm.
medium

Multiple product types with no logical link

The factory produces very different product categories that would normally require different equipment, skills, or supply chains, with no clear explanation of how they manage all in-house.
medium

Storage and logistics anomalies

Large volumes of finished goods arrive pre-packed or on pallets that don’t match the factory’s internal packaging line; shipping marks or carton styles differ from what’s produced on-site.
ecqa
+1

Staff cannot explain processes in detail

Line leaders or supervisors struggle to describe key steps, tooling, or process parameters for “their” products, suggesting they’re not the real manufacturer.
tradeaiders

Walk the full flow: raw materials → production → QC → packaging → warehouse. Any broken link or unexplained handover point is a potential subcontracting node.

4. Ownership, Branding, and Site Overlap

Unauthorised subcontractors often share hidden connections with the audited factory:

Shared facilities or staff across sites

Workers, dormitories, canteens, or equipment are shared between the audited factory and another site, but that second site is not disclosed or included in the audit scope.
medium

Common ownership or management not declared

Business names, stamps, or contracts reveal the same owners or management team running multiple facilities, but only one is presented as “the factory”.
medium

Products too similar across “different” factories

Another site produces near-identical products with the same moulds, labels, or packaging, indicating they’re effectively part of the same supply chain but kept off the audit.
medium

For due diligence, cross-check the business licence, factory address, and company name on contracts, invoices, and compliance certificates; mismatches often indicate undisclosed sites.

5. Behavioural Red Flags During the Audit

How the factory responds to your questions can itself be a warning sign:

Resistance to full access

Reluctance to show certain areas (raw material store, mould room, specific lines), or insistence that some areas are “under maintenance” or “not relevant”.

Practical Steps for an Australian Buyer

To specifically protect yourself as an Australian importer:

Contractual controls

Include explicit clauses prohibiting unauthorised subcontracting and requiring written approval for any outsourced process. Specify that all subcontractors must meet the same compliance and quality standards.
medium

Audit scope clarity

State in your audit protocol that all production sites involved in your orders must be disclosed and are subject to audit. Make it clear that hidden factories are unacceptable.
medium

Traceability requirements

Require batch-level traceability from raw materials to finished goods, with documentation you can spot-check during audits and pre-shipment inspections.
wadhsons

Use independent auditors/inspectors

Engage third-party auditors experienced in detecting hidden factories and subcontracting in your product category (toys, electronics, textiles, etc.).

Cross-check with Australian compliance needs

Tie subcontracting controls to your Australian regulatory obligations (product safety, ACCC requirements, modern slavery due diligence) so the factory understands the legal stakes for you.

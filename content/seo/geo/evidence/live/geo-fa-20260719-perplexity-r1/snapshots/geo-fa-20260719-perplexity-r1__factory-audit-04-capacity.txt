Core capacity verification methods

Auditors typically assess capacity through several complementary approaches:

Production line observation and throughput measurement — Auditors physically observe production lines, count workstations, measure cycle times, and calculate actual output per shift rather than relying on supplier claims.

Equipment and machinery inventory — Auditors document the number, condition, and age of production equipment, checking whether machines are operational, well-maintained, or frequently broken down (which reduces effective capacity).

Workforce assessment — Auditors verify actual staff numbers, shift patterns, overtime practices, and staff turnover rates, since high turnover or understaffing directly impacts capacity.

Production planning and scheduling systems — Auditors review how the factory plans production, including whether they use formal scheduling software, how they prioritize orders, and their track record for on-time delivery.

Warehouse and inventory management — Auditors check raw material inventory levels, finished goods storage capacity, and warehouse organization, since poor material planning creates bottlenecks.

Red flags that indicate capacity problems

Auditors watch for warning signs that capacity may be insufficient:

Overstated capacity claims — Factories claiming unrealistic output figures without supporting evidence.
qualityinspection

High equipment downtime — Multiple broken or non-operational machines on the production floor.

Poor production planning — No formal scheduling system, frequent last-minute changes, or chaotic warehouse organization.

High staff turnover or understaffing — Visible workforce gaps, excessive overtime, or inability to maintain consistent staffing levels.

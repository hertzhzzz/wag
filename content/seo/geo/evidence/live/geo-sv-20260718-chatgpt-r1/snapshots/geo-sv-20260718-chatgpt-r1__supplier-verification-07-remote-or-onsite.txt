## Prompt (exact)
When is remote supplier verification enough, and when should an Australian buyer commission an on-site check?

## User message (UI)
When is remote supplier verification enough, and when should an Australian buyer commission an on-site check?

## Assistant answer (UI text)
For an Australian buyer importing from China (or another overseas supplier), remote supplier verification is usually enough for low-risk, repeat, or small-value purchases, but an on-site inspection becomes justified when the financial, compliance, or operational risk is significant.

Remote verification is usually enough when:
1. The order value is low

Examples:

Trial order under ~A$5,000–10,000

Small quantities

Non-critical products

Remote checks can often confirm:

Legal company existence

Export capability

Product documentation

Basic manufacturing capability

Banking details

2. The supplier is already proven

Remote verification may be sufficient if:

You have successfully completed multiple shipments

Quality has been consistent

Payment history is reliable

The supplier has long-term customers or references

3. The product has low compliance risk

Examples:

Simple consumer goods

Non-regulated accessories

Standard components

Less need for physical verification if failure would not create major liability.

4. The supplier provides strong documentation

Useful remote evidence includes:

Chinese business licence

Unified Social Credit Code verification

Export licence or customs registration evidence

Factory address and production photos/videos

Product certifications

Test reports from recognised laboratories

Customer references

Video call from the factory floor

Bank account matching the registered company name

An on-site check is recommended when:
1. The order value is high

Consider an inspection when:

First order exceeds ~A$20,000–50,000

Large deposit is required

Custom tooling or moulds are involved

Long-term supply agreement is planned

A single factory visit can prevent losses from:

Fake factories

Trading companies pretending to manufacture

Misrepresented capacity

Poor quality systems

2. Product compliance risk is high

For Australia, consider on-site verification for products involving:

Electrical equipment

Lighting products

AV equipment

Batteries

Machinery

Children's products

Safety equipment

Products requiring Australian certifications

Examples:

A supplier claims RCM compliance but cannot show test reports

Certificates belong to another company/model

The factory cannot explain production controls

3. The supplier claims to be a manufacturer

A common issue is a trading company presenting itself as a factory.

On-site checks can verify:

Actual production lines

Machinery ownership

Number of workers

Assembly capability

Quality control department

Raw material storage

Packaging process

Warning signs requiring inspection:

Very broad product catalogue

No factory photos

Refuses video calls

Factory address changes frequently

Prices significantly below competitors

4. You need production assurance

Remote verification cannot reliably confirm:

Whether your order is actually being produced

Whether materials match specifications

Whether QC procedures exist

Whether subcontracting is occurring

For important orders, use:

Factory verification before payment

Production inspection during manufacturing

Pre-shipment inspection before final payment

5. The supplier requests risky payment terms

On-site checks are strongly recommended when:

100% payment upfront is requested

Payment goes to a personal account

Payment goes to a different company

Supplier refuses escrow or trade assurance mechanisms

The supplier is newly established

Practical decision framework
Situation	Recommended approach
Sample order under A$5k	Remote verification
Repeat supplier, proven history	Remote verification + periodic audits
First order A$10k–50k	Remote verification + consider factory audit
First order above A$50k	On-site inspection recommended
Custom tooling/moulds	On-site verification
Electrical/safety products	On-site or third-party audit
Supplier claims own factory	Verify physically
Large annual contract	Initial audit + annual review
Minimum verification package before sending money

For most Australian importers, the minimum should include:

Chinese company registration check

Legal Chinese name

Unified Social Credit Code

Registered address

Business scope

Ownership and payment verification

Bank account matches company name

No unexplained third-party payments

Factory capability evidence

Live video call

Production line footage

Equipment list

Employee count

Export evidence

Customs registration

Previous export records where possible

Product compliance verification

Test reports

Certification ownership

Model numbers matching your product

A useful rule for Australian importers is:

Remote verification proves the supplier exists.
An on-site inspection proves the supplier can actually deliver.

For a first-time supplier relationship involving significant money, regulated products, or a long-term partnership, an on-site check is usually cheaper than recovering from a failed shipment.

## User message (UI)
What supplier verification findings should stop an Australian buyer from proceeding with a purchase order?

## Assistant answer (UI text)
An Australian buyer should generally stop or pause issuing a purchase order if supplier verification reveals issues that materially increase the risk of fraud, non-delivery, regulatory breaches, or inability to enforce the contract.

Immediate stop conditions (red flags)
Finding	Why it should stop the purchase
Bank account does not match the verified legal entity	Common indicator of payment fraud, fake suppliers, or unauthorised intermediaries. Do not pay until ownership is independently verified.
Supplier refuses to provide official business registration details	A legitimate exporter should be able to provide its Chinese legal name, Unified Social Credit Code, business licence, and registered address.
Business licence information cannot be verified on official Chinese government databases	May indicate forged documents, an inactive company, or a fake identity.
Company name on contract, invoice, bank account, and certificates do not match	Creates enforcement and payment disputes. Require a documented relationship (e.g., authorised trading company agreement).
Supplier asks for payment to a personal account or unrelated offshore account	High fraud risk and often inconsistent with normal business practice.
Supplier refuses video call, factory inspection, or basic verification	Especially concerning for high-value orders or products requiring manufacturing capability.
Factory claims cannot be confirmed	If the supplier presents itself as a manufacturer but verification shows only an office, apartment, or unrelated location, reassess.
Certificates appear altered, copied, expired, or unverifiable	Could expose the buyer to Australian compliance failures, recalls, or customs problems.
Supplier cannot prove export capability	Lack of export registration, customs history, or experience can indicate a broker or inexperienced entity.
Unrealistically low pricing compared with market suppliers	Often indicates counterfeit goods, lower specifications, hidden costs, or fraud.
Pressure to pay quickly before verification is complete	Fraudulent suppliers commonly create urgency.
Supplier changes bank details shortly before payment	Treat as a potential business email compromise (BEC) event until independently confirmed.
Product compliance findings that should stop the order

For regulated or safety-sensitive products, stop if the supplier cannot provide:

Valid test reports from recognised laboratories.

Correct Australian compliance documentation where required.

Evidence that certificates apply to the exact model being purchased.

Traceable product specifications.

Material declarations where relevant.

Examples:

Electrical equipment: missing or questionable RCM-related evidence.

Wireless products: missing Australian radio compliance evidence.

Safety equipment: missing applicable certification.

Children's products: missing required safety documentation.

Manufacturing capability findings

Stop or require further investigation if:

The factory address differs from the address shown in documents.

The supplier cannot show production equipment, workers, or quality-control processes.

Production capacity claims are unrealistic.

The supplier refuses to identify subcontractors.

Previous customer references cannot be verified.

Samples are significantly different from production commitments.

Legal and ownership concerns

Do not proceed if:

The company has abnormal legal status (for example, cancelled, revoked, or inactive registration).

Ownership information is inconsistent.

The supplier has repeated legal disputes related to fraud, contracts, or unpaid debts.

The supplier refuses written contracts governed by clear terms.

Payment-related stop rules

Before paying a deposit:

Verify the beneficiary name independently using contact details already known, not only email instructions.

Confirm payment instructions by phone/video with a verified company representative.

Avoid changing payment destinations based solely on email messages.

A common safer structure for first orders:

Small trial order.

Third-party inspection before shipment.

Controlled payment milestones.

Balance payment only after inspection approval.

A practical decision rule

An Australian importer should usually not proceed when any of these occur:

❌ Identity cannot be independently verified

❌ Payment recipient cannot be verified

❌ Compliance documents cannot be validated

❌ Supplier refuses reasonable verification

❌ Manufacturing claims cannot be proven

❌ The supplier identity changes during negotiations

One major unresolved red flag is often enough to delay the purchase order; multiple minor inconsistencies should be treated as a combined risk signal.

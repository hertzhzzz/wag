When a China quality inspection fails, the buyer should treat the failure as a production control issue requiring evidence-based corrective action, rather than immediately accepting or rejecting the shipment. The correct response depends on the severity of defects, order timing, contract terms, and supplier history.

1. Review the inspection report carefully

Confirm:

Inspection stage

During-production inspection (DUPRO)

Pre-shipment inspection (PSI)

Container loading inspection (CLI)

Defect classification

Critical defects

Major defects

Minor defects

AQL results

Number of samples checked

Defect quantities found

Whether the lot exceeded agreed AQL limits

Evidence

Photos

Videos

Measurements

Test results

Packaging checks

Do not rely only on the inspector's conclusion; verify whether the findings relate to agreed specifications.

2. Stop final payment if contract terms allow

For most China imports:

Do not release the remaining balance until the supplier provides an acceptable resolution.

Do not approve shipment based only on supplier promises.

Ensure payment terms include inspection acceptance conditions.

Common payment structure:

30% deposit before production

70% after passing inspection

A failed inspection should normally pause the 70% payment.

3. Categorise the failure
Minor issues

Examples:

Cosmetic marks

Small packaging inconsistencies

Label placement errors

Possible actions:

Accept with discount

Request rework

Require correction before shipment

Major issues

Examples:

Wrong dimensions

Missing components

Poor assembly

Functional failures

Incorrect materials

Possible actions:

Supplier rework

Replace defective units

Repeat inspection after correction

Critical issues

Examples:

Safety failures

Electrical hazards

Non-compliance with Australian regulations

Counterfeit materials

Dangerous defects

Possible actions:

Reject shipment

Cancel order

Escalate supplier investigation

4. Request a corrective action plan (CAP)

The supplier should provide:

Root cause analysis

Why did the defect happen?

Corrective action

What will be changed?

Preventive action

How will recurrence be prevented?

Completion timeline

When will rework finish?

Example:

Problem	Cause	Correction
Incorrect wiring	Worker used old specification	Update production drawings and retrain workers
Scratched surfaces	Poor packaging protection	Add protective film and revise packing method
5. Decide whether to re-inspect

After correction:

Arrange a follow-up inspection.

Use the same checklist and AQL standard.

Confirm that defects have actually been fixed.

Do not rely on factory photos alone.

A second inspection is especially important for:

First production orders

Large-value shipments

Custom products

Regulated goods

6. Negotiate the commercial solution

Possible outcomes:

Accept shipment with compensation

Suitable when:

Defects are usable

Repair cost is low

Market timing is important

Examples:

Price reduction

Free replacement parts

Credit note on next order

Require rework

Suitable when:

Product can be corrected before export

Supplier has enough production time

Reject shipment

Suitable when:

Safety issues exist

Defect rate is high

Supplier cannot provide reliable correction

7. Investigate whether the problem is supplier or specification related

A failed inspection may reveal:

Supplier problems

Poor factory controls

Subcontracting

Weak quality management

Production shortcuts

Buyer problems

Unclear specifications

Missing drawings

No approved samples

Ambiguous packaging requirements

Update the product specification and inspection checklist before the next order.

8. Record the failure for supplier performance tracking

Maintain:

Inspection reports

Defect photos

Corrective action records

Supplier responses

Final resolution

This creates a supplier scorecard covering:

Quality performance

Delivery reliability

Communication

Corrective action speed

Recommended decision framework for Australian importers
Situation	Recommended action
Minor defects within acceptable limits	Accept or negotiate compensation
AQL failure but repairable	Require rework + reinspection
Major functional defects	Hold payment and investigate
Safety/compliance failure	Reject shipment
Repeated failures	Reassess or replace supplier

For Australian buyers importing from China, a failed inspection should usually trigger a controlled correction and verification process, not an immediate shipment rejection or acceptance. The goal is to prevent defective goods from leaving China while preserving a workable supplier relationship when the supplier can demonstrate effective corrective action.

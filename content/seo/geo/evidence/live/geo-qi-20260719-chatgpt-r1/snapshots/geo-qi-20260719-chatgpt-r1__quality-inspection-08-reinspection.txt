Reinspection is necessary after a factory claims defects have been corrected when the buyer needs independent confirmation that the corrective action was completed and the goods now meet the agreed requirements. A factory’s internal confirmation, photos, or written assurances are usually not sufficient for high-risk or high-value orders.

Reinspection is recommended when:
1. The original inspection failed

If a pre-shipment inspection (PSI), during-production inspection, or final random inspection found defects above the agreed AQL limits, a reinspection should normally be performed before shipment approval.

Example:

Original inspection: 8 major defects found, exceeding the allowed limit.

Factory claims all defects were repaired.

Buyer arranges reinspection before releasing final payment.

2. Defects affect product safety or compliance

Reinspection is strongly recommended for issues involving:

Electrical safety

Batteries

Machinery operation

Child safety

Food-contact materials

Chemical compliance

Australian regulatory requirements

A simple factory statement that the issue was fixed is insufficient because a hidden production problem may remain.

3. The factory performed extensive rework

If the factory:

Replaced large quantities of components

Reassembled products

Reworked packaging

Changed suppliers

Modified production processes

the buyer should verify that the rework did not introduce new defects.

4. The order value is significant

For expensive shipments, the cost of inspection is usually small compared with the risk of receiving unusable goods.

Common triggers:

Large purchase orders

First production run

New supplier relationship

Custom products

Private-label goods

5. The factory only provides evidence instead of physical verification

Photos and videos can help, but they may not prove:

All units were corrected

Defects were not hidden

Correct replacement parts were used

Packaging meets requirements

An independent inspector should check actual production samples.

Situations where reinspection may not be necessary

Reinspection may be unnecessary if:

The issue was purely documentary (for example, missing paperwork)

The defect was minor and easily verified remotely

The buyer has a trusted long-term supplier with a proven corrective-action system

The factory provides strong evidence and the shipment risk is low

Recommended reinspection process

Factory submits corrective action report

Root cause

Quantity affected

Repair/replacement method

Preventive measures

Buyer defines acceptance criteria

Same checklist as the original inspection

Same AQL levels

Same critical/major/minor defect limits

Inspector verifies

Corrected units

Random samples from the entire batch

Packaging and labelling

Functional testing where applicable

Buyer approves shipment only after passing results

For Australian importers, a practical rule is: if the original failure could affect customer complaints, returns, safety, compliance, or brand reputation, arrange reinspection before shipment.

An importer should create a product-specific inspection checklist by converting the product requirements, supplier agreement, and quality risks into measurable inspection points. A generic checklist only checks broad items (appearance, quantity, packaging); a product-specific checklist verifies whether the goods actually meet the buyer’s expectations.

1. Start with the product specification sheet

Collect all approved product information:

Product drawings or CAD files

Technical specifications

Material requirements

Approved samples (“golden sample”)

Colour references (Pantone/RAL codes if relevant)

Packaging requirements

Labelling requirements

Compliance documents

User manuals or installation instructions

Purchase order requirements

Every inspection point should trace back to one of these documents.

2. Divide the checklist into inspection categories

A typical structure:

Category	Product-specific examples
Quantity	Carton count, unit count, spare parts included
Product appearance	Surface finish, scratches, colour consistency, stitching quality
Dimensions	Length, width, thickness, tolerance limits
Materials	Grade of steel, fabric composition, plastic type
Function testing	Power-on test, load test, water resistance test, movement test
Safety	Sharp edges, insulation, warning labels
Assembly	Screw torque, alignment, component fit
Packaging	Carton strength, foam protection, barcode placement
Documentation	Certificates, manuals, warranty cards
3. Convert requirements into measurable inspection points

Avoid vague checks.

Weak checklist item:

Check product quality.

Better checklist items:

Verify aluminium frame thickness is 2.0 ± 0.1 mm using a calliper.

Test each unit’s power switch for 20 activation cycles.

Confirm product colour matches approved sample under daylight lighting.

Measure product dimensions against drawing tolerance ±3 mm.

4. Define defect standards before inspection

Create acceptance rules:

Critical defects

Safety or legal issues:

Electrical safety failure

Incorrect certification markings

Dangerous sharp edges

Wrong product model

Usually:

Zero tolerance

Major defects

Affects usability or customer satisfaction:

Product does not function correctly

Incorrect materials

Visible damage

Missing components

Example:

AQL 2.5

Minor defects

Cosmetic issues:

Small scratches

Slight packaging damage

Minor colour variation

Example:

AQL 4.0

5. Include product-specific testing procedures

Examples:

Electronics

Voltage compatibility

Charging test

Battery capacity test

Screen/panel inspection

Firmware version verification

Furniture

Load-bearing test

Stability test

Surface finish inspection

Hardware completeness

Lighting equipment

Power-on test

Brightness verification

Colour temperature check

DMX/control testing

Cable and connector inspection

Machinery

Operational cycle test

Noise/vibration test

Safety guard inspection

Hydraulic/pneumatic pressure test

6. Add sampling requirements

Specify:

Inspection level (for example, ANSI/ASQ Z1.4)

Sample quantity

Random sampling method

AQL limits

Example:

Inspect 200 units from a 5,000-unit shipment using General Inspection Level II, AQL 1.5 major defects and 4.0 minor defects.

7. Include photo evidence requirements

The checklist should require inspectors to capture:

Overall product photos

Defect close-ups

Measurement photos

Test result photos

Packaging condition

Shipping marks

This helps resolve disputes with suppliers.

8. Match the checklist to the production stage

Different stages need different checklists:

Stage	Focus
Pre-production	Materials, samples, supplier capability
During production	Process control, workmanship, early defects
Pre-shipment inspection	Finished product quality, quantity, packaging
Container loading	Correct cartons, loading condition, sealing
9. Have the factory approve the checklist before production

Before mass production:

Send the checklist to the supplier.

Confirm they understand requirements.

Update unclear points.

Freeze the final version.

Use the same checklist for inspection.

This reduces arguments such as “we did not know this was required.”

For an Australian importer buying from China, the strongest approach is usually a three-document system:

Product specification sheet — what the product must be.

Inspection checklist — how compliance is measured.

Defect standard/AQL plan — what level of failure is acceptable.

This creates an objective basis for approving or rejecting a shipment before releasing final payment.

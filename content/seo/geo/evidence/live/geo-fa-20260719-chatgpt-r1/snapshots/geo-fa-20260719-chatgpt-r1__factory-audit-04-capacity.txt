A capacity assessment should determine whether the factory can realistically produce the buyer's order without causing delays, quality problems, or excessive subcontracting. It should rely on production records and direct observations rather than management assurances.

1. Compare Available Capacity with Required Capacity

Start with the buyer's requirements:

Product specification

Order quantity

Required delivery date

Expected production start date

Then calculate whether the factory has sufficient available production time.

For example:

Item	Example
Order quantity	80,000 units
Standard output	2,500 units/day
Production days required	32 days
Days available before shipment	40 days
Existing committed production	18 days

In this example, only 22 production days remain available, suggesting the factory may not be able to meet the delivery schedule.

2. Review Current Production Loading

Request:

Master production schedule

Production planning board

ERP/MRP schedule

Machine loading schedule

Line allocation schedule

Check:

Which customers are already booked

Peak production periods

Overtime assumptions

Planned maintenance shutdowns

National holidays

Seasonal production spikes

An overloaded schedule is often the strongest indicator of delivery risk.

3. Verify Production Records

Review records for the previous 3–12 months.

Examples include:

Daily production reports

Line output reports

Machine utilization reports

Shift reports

Production efficiency reports

Downtime records

Ask:

What is actual daily output?

How much does production fluctuate?

Are claimed capacities regularly achieved?

Actual historical output is generally more reliable than theoretical capacity.

4. Inspect Production Lines

Observe:

Number of active lines

Number of idle lines

Staffing levels

Machine condition

Bottlenecks

Work-in-progress inventory

A factory claiming ten production lines but operating only six may have substantially lower practical capacity.

5. Assess Labour Availability

Review:

Employee roster

Headcount

Attendance records

Turnover rate

Temporary labour usage

Recruitment plans

Questions include:

Can additional shifts be staffed?

Is skilled labour available?

Are key operators shared between production lines?

High labour turnover or dependence on temporary workers can reduce effective capacity.

6. Evaluate Equipment Capacity

Review:

Equipment list

Critical machine quantities

Preventive maintenance records

Breakdown logs

Machine utilisation

Look for bottlenecks such as:

Only one CNC machine

One powder coating line

One test laboratory

One packaging line

The slowest process typically determines overall factory capacity.

7. Check Material Supply Readiness

Production capacity is meaningful only if materials are available.

Review:

Raw material inventory

Supplier lead times

Purchasing plans

Safety stock

Long-lead components

A factory with sufficient production resources but delayed material deliveries may still miss shipment deadlines.

8. Review Delivery Performance

Past performance often predicts future performance.

Request:

On-time delivery KPI

Customer complaints

Late shipment records

Corrective actions

Major production delays

Consistently poor delivery performance may indicate chronic capacity constraints.

9. Identify Hidden Capacity Risks

Watch for indicators such as:

Excessive overtime every month

Frequent weekend production

Constant expedited shipping

Large work-in-progress queues

Significant unfinished orders

Heavy reliance on subcontractors

Frequent schedule changes

These suggest the factory is already operating at or near its limits.

10. Cross-Check Management Claims

Factory management may report optimistic figures. Verify by comparing:

Management Claim	Independent Evidence
Monthly capacity	Historical production reports
Machine quantity	Physical inspection
Number of workers	Payroll and attendance records
Available production slots	Current production schedule
On-time delivery	Shipping records
Production efficiency	Daily output reports

Significant discrepancies warrant further investigation.

Capacity Assessment Outcome

An audit should conclude with a risk-based assessment, for example:

Risk Level	Characteristics
Low	Demonstrated spare capacity, stable workforce, strong delivery history, realistic production plan
Medium	Capacity is sufficient but depends on overtime, efficiency improvements, or tight scheduling
High	Existing commitments exceed available capacity, key bottlenecks exist, or historical delivery performance is poor

For an Australian importer placing a substantial order, the most reliable capacity assessment combines document review, shop-floor observation, historical production data, and production planning analysis. Capacity should be based on verified achievable output, not the factory's stated maximum or theoretical production figures.

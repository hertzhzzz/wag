A production audit in China should go beyond checking whether documents exist. The auditor should verify that records are authentic, current, internally consistent, and match what is observed on the factory floor. Discrepancies between documentation and actual operations are often one of the strongest indicators of quality or supply-chain risk.

Company and legal records

Review documents that confirm the factory's identity and ability to manufacture the products:

Chinese Business Licence (营业执照)

Unified Social Credit Code

Manufacturing scope and registered business activities

Export registration (if the factory exports directly)

Factory organisation chart

Key management contacts

Production site ownership or lease documentation

The legal company name should match purchase contracts, invoices, bank accounts, and quality documentation.

Quality management documentation

Request evidence that the factory operates a documented quality system rather than relying solely on experience.

Typical documents include:

Quality manual

Quality procedures

Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs)

Inspection standards

Incoming Quality Control (IQC) procedures

In-Process Quality Control (IPQC) procedures

Final Quality Control (FQC) procedures

Corrective and Preventive Action (CAPA) records

Internal audit reports

Management review records

Customer complaint logs

Non-conformance reports (NCRs)

An auditor should also verify whether these documents are actively used by production staff.

Production planning records

These demonstrate whether the factory has sufficient production control.

Examples include:

Production schedules

Manufacturing orders

Work orders

Capacity planning reports

Daily production reports

Machine loading plans

Delivery schedules

Production KPI reports

The auditor should compare scheduled production against actual production.

Raw material records

Review documentation covering material control from receipt through consumption.

Typical records include:

Approved supplier list

Material specifications

Incoming inspection reports

Material receiving records

Batch identification records

Warehouse inventory

FIFO implementation records

Material traceability records

The auditor should confirm that material batches can be traced into finished products.

Process control documentation

Each manufacturing process should have documented controls.

Examples:

Process flow charts

Work instructions

Control plans

Process parameters

Machine setup sheets

Changeover records

Operator instructions

First Article Inspection (FAI) records

The documented process should match what operators actually do.

Inspection and testing records

Inspection records demonstrate whether quality checks are consistently performed.

These commonly include:

Incoming inspection reports

Patrol inspection records

In-process inspection records

Final inspection reports

Outgoing inspection reports

Functional test reports

Dimensional inspection reports

Sample retention records

Product release approvals

Review multiple production lots rather than only the most recent batch.

Equipment maintenance records

Reliable production depends on maintained equipment.

Review:

Equipment register

Preventive maintenance schedules

Maintenance logs

Repair records

Machine downtime records

Spare parts records

Equipment qualification records

Poor maintenance often correlates with inconsistent product quality.

Calibration records

Any equipment used for measurement should have current calibration documentation.

Examples:

Calibration certificates

Calibration schedules

Gauge register

Gauge identification labels

Out-of-tolerance investigations

Expired calibration certificates are a common audit finding.

Personnel and training records

Production quality depends heavily on operator competence.

Review:

Training matrix

Operator qualification records

Training attendance

Skills assessments

Special process certifications (where applicable)

Interview operators to verify that training has been understood and applied.

Traceability records

A capable factory should be able to trace products through production.

Typical records include:

Batch numbers

Production dates

Operator identification

Machine identification

Material batch linkage

Finished goods traceability

Shipping records

The auditor should perform a traceability exercise from finished product back to raw materials.

Purchasing and supplier management

If critical components are outsourced, review:

Supplier approval process

Supplier evaluations

Supplier audit reports

Supplier performance metrics

Incoming defect trends

Corrective action requests issued to suppliers

Environmental and safety records

Depending on the product and audit scope:

Hazardous chemical registers

Waste disposal records

Fire inspection records

Emergency drill records

Equipment safety inspections

PPE training records

Product-specific compliance documentation

Where relevant:

Product specifications

Engineering drawings

Bills of Materials (BOM)

Customer-approved samples

Regulatory compliance certificates

Test reports

Packaging specifications

Label approvals

Audit techniques

Rather than checking documents in isolation, an auditor should cross-reference them with physical evidence. For example:

Record reviewed	Verify on the factory floor
Production order	Product currently being manufactured
Inspection report	Actual inspection activity and records
Calibration certificate	Measuring instrument label and serial number
Maintenance log	Machine condition and maintenance stickers
Training record	Operator knowledge and correct procedure
Material receiving record	Warehouse inventory and batch labels
Traceability record	Finished goods, work-in-progress, and raw materials

The strongest audits combine document review, factory observation, employee interviews, and record traceability. If records appear complete but cannot be matched to actual production practices, or if different documents contain inconsistent dates, quantities, or company details, those discrepancies warrant further investigation before placing significant purchase orders.

Before placing a large purchase order with a Chinese supplier, an Australian importer should commission a factory audit that verifies not only that the supplier exists, but also that it has the capability, legal status, quality systems, and financial stability to fulfil the order consistently.

A comprehensive pre-order factory audit should cover the following areas:

Area	What should be verified	Why it matters
Legal identity	Chinese legal name, Unified Social Credit Code, registered address, business scope, legal representative	Confirms you are contracting with the correct legal entity
Manufacturing capability	Whether the supplier is an actual manufacturer or a trading company, factory ownership, production lines	Ensures production will occur where claimed
Export capability	Export licence (where applicable), customs registration, previous export history	Confirms the supplier can legally export your products
Factory facilities	Buildings, machinery, maintenance, utilities, warehouse conditions	Indicates production capacity and operational maturity
Production capacity	Monthly output, peak capacity, bottlenecks, overtime reliance, subcontracting	Determines whether your order can be completed on time
Workforce	Number of employees, key technical staff, training, turnover	High turnover can affect product quality and delivery
Quality management	Incoming inspection, in-process inspection, final inspection, calibration, corrective actions	Demonstrates whether quality is managed systematically
Certifications	ISO 9001, ISO 14001, ISO 45001 or relevant industry certifications	Certificates should be independently verified
Supply chain	Major raw material suppliers, traceability, supplier approval process	Reduces risk of inconsistent materials
Product experience	Similar products manufactured previously, major export markets, customer references	Experience generally reduces production risk
Equipment	Age, maintenance records, testing equipment, calibration status	Modern, maintained equipment generally produces more consistent results
Laboratory/testing	In-house testing capability or accredited external laboratories	Important for products requiring compliance testing
Compliance	Environmental permits, labour compliance, workplace safety	Reduces legal and reputational risk
Financial indicators	Years in business, litigation, unusual ownership changes, serious credit issues	Helps identify financially unstable suppliers
Intellectual property controls	Confidentiality procedures, access controls, customer-specific production management	Important when supplying proprietary products
Quality system assessment

Rather than simply checking whether the supplier has an ISO certificate, the audit should determine whether quality procedures are actually being followed. Examples include:

Incoming material inspection records

Process control documentation

Non-conformance reports

Corrective and preventive action (CAPA) records

Internal audit records

Calibration schedules

Batch traceability

Staff training records

A factory with documented procedures but poor implementation presents a significant risk.

Production capability

The auditor should assess whether the supplier can realistically produce your required volume.

Typical questions include:

What percentage of capacity is already committed?

How many production lines are available?

Are additional shifts required?

Is subcontracting used?

What is the current lead time?

How are production schedules managed?

This helps identify suppliers that accept orders beyond their true capacity.

Red flags

Findings that warrant further investigation include:

Factory address differs from registration documents.

Production occurs primarily through undisclosed subcontractors.

Machinery is inconsistent with the claimed production volume.

Large areas of the factory appear unused.

No documented quality records.

Employee count is far below claimed capacity.

Certificates cannot be independently verified.

Frequent recent changes to company ownership or legal representative.

Poor warehouse organisation and inadequate product identification.

Refusal to allow access to production areas.

A combination of multiple red flags should be treated as a significant commercial risk.

Products requiring Australian regulatory compliance

If the products will be sold in Australia, the audit should also confirm the supplier understands and can support applicable Australian requirements, such as:

Product testing requirements

Technical documentation

Material declarations

Traceability

Packaging and labelling requirements

Ongoing consistency of production after approval

Where applicable, confirm that test reports relate to the actual factory and current product specification rather than older or unrelated products.

Deliverables

A professional factory audit report should typically include:

Executive risk summary

Legal verification results

Photographs of facilities and production lines

Equipment inventory

Production capacity assessment

Quality management evaluation

Compliance findings

Identified risks and non-conformities

Recommended corrective actions

Overall supplier risk rating (for example, low, medium, or high)

For a large Australian import order, a factory audit is usually only one element of supplier due diligence. It is best combined with legal entity verification, independent confirmation of bank account details, export capability verification, pre-production sample approval, and pre-shipment inspection to reduce the risk of fraud, quality failures, or supply disruptions.

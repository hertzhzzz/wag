How to set an AQL standard and defect limits for a China production order

AQL (Acceptable Quality Limit) is the statistical sampling standard used in China manufacturing to decide whether a shipment passes or fails inspection. You set it by choosing defect categories, AQL values for each, an inspection level, and then writing these explicitly into your purchase contract so a third‑party inspector can apply them without ambiguity.

1. Understand what AQL actually means

AQL is not “the shipment will have at most X% defects.”
linkedin

It means: if the true defect rate is at or below the AQL you set, the lot has a high probability (about 95%) of passing the sampling inspection.
linkedin

Inspectors draw a random sample from your total batch using ISO 2859‑1 tables, then compare the number of defects found to the Accept/Reject (Ac/Re) limits for your chosen AQL.

2. Classify your defects before picking AQL numbers

Work with your supplier early and define three tiers of defects for your product:

Critical defects

Safety or regulatory failures (sharp edges on toys, electrical hazards, prohibited chemicals, mislabelled safety warnings).

Usually: AQL 0 (no critical defects allowed).

+1

Major defects

Functional or durability issues likely to cause returns/complaints (broken parts, wrong size, major colour mismatch, missing components).

Typical range: AQL 1.0–2.5, depending on how strict your brand needs to be.
linkedin
+1

Minor defects

Cosmetic or small deviations unlikely to cause returns (small scratches, slight colour variation, minor packaging misalignment).

Typical range: AQL 2.5–4.0 for general consumer goods.
linkedin
+1

Write these as a defect classification list in your contract/spec sheet, with examples and photos so an inspector can judge consistently.

3. Choose your AQL values and inspection level

A common, defensible starting point for many consumer goods sourced from China:

Critical defects: AQL 0

Major defects: AQL 2.5

Minor defects: AQL 4.0

Inspection level: General Inspection Level II (GII) – the industry default.

+1

Adjust tighter or looser based on:

Product risk:

Electronics, baby products, toys, anything with safety/liability → tighten major to AQL 1.0 or even 0 for some attributes.
linkedin

Low‑risk items (simple packaging, promotional goods) → you might accept higher minor AQL.

Your quality expectations and price point:

Premium brand / high unit price → tighter AQLs.

Very low‑margin, high‑volume commodity → sometimes accept slightly higher defect rates if financially justified.

Always specify the full formula in your contract, e.g.:

“AQL 0 / 2.5 / 4.0, General Inspection Level II, single sampling plan, per ISO 2859‑1.”

4. Use the AQL tables to determine sample size and pass/fail limits

Once you know:

Total order quantity (lot size)

Chosen inspection level (usually GII)

Chosen AQL values for each defect tier

you use the ISO 2859‑1 AQL tables:

Find the lot size range in Table 1 (e.g. 501–1,200 units).

Select your inspection level (GII) to get the sample size code letter (e.g. “J”).
examinechina

Use that letter in Table 2 to get:

Sample size (e.g. 80 units for code J)

Accept (Ac) and Reject (Re) numbers for each AQL (e.g. for AQL 2.5 and sample 80, maybe Ac = 5, Re = 6).
linkedin
+1

Practical rule of thumb:

Larger orders → larger samples, but the percentage sampled drops.

Stricter AQLs → lower Ac numbers, so fewer defects are tolerated before rejection.

You don’t need to memorize the tables; most inspection agencies provide an AQL calculator or will do this for you if you specify the AQL and inspection level.

5. Bake AQL into your purchase order / contract

To make AQL enforceable:

State the standard clearly in the PO or contract:

AQL values (critical/major/minor)

Inspection level (e.g. GII)

Sampling plan type (single, normal)

Reference to ISO 2859‑1 or ANSI/ASQC Z1.4.

Tie payment to inspection results:

Hold back final payment (commonly 20–30%) until a pre‑shipment inspection passes.

Make the inspection certificate or your written release a condition for shipment.

Define consequences of failure:

Add protection for latent/field failures:

State that passing AQL does not waive claims for hidden or later‑emerging defects.

Include an “epidemic failure” clause: if field defect rate exceeds, say, 2–3% within warranty, supplier bears replacements, freight, and a defined share of costs.

6. Use a golden sample and detailed spec sheet

AQL sampling only works if the inspector knows what “defect” means for your product:

Golden sample:

Signed and stamped by both parties.

One copy with you, one with the factory.

Referenced by number in the contract.

Specification sheet (contract annex):

Materials, dimensions and tolerances, Pantone colours, packaging, labelling.

Applicable safety standards (EN 71, CE, FCC, etc., as needed).

Defect list with examples:

Photos or clear descriptions of what counts as critical/major/minor for your specific item.

7. Plan inspections at the right time

During production (DUPRO):

Inspect when 20–40% of units are complete to catch issues early, before full production.

Pre‑shipment inspection (PSI):

After 100% production and at least 80% packed, before goods leave the factory.

8. Example: setting AQL for a 5,000‑unit order

Suppose you’re ordering 5,000 home‑goods units:

Decide defect tiers:

Critical: safety issues → AQL 0

Major: functional defects, breakage → AQL 2.5

Minor: cosmetic issues → AQL 4.0

Choose: General Inspection Level II, single sampling.

Using AQL tables (or an inspector’s calculator):

For 5,000 units at GII, you might get a sample size around 200 units.
milasourcing

For AQL 2.5 major on 200 samples, the lot might pass if ≤ 10 major defects are found, and fail at 11 or more.
milasourcing

For AQL 4.0 minor, you might allow more minor defects before failing.

You then write into your PO:

“Pre‑shipment inspection: AQL 0 / 2.5 / 4.0, General Inspection Level II, single sampling, ISO 2859‑1. Final 30% payment upon passed inspection. Supplier bears rework and re‑inspection costs if failed.”

9. Common mistakes to avoid

Using only “AQL 2.5” without defining critical/major/minor or inspection level. This invites disputes.
redflagsolutions

Assuming AQL 2.5 guarantees ≤ 2.5% defects in the whole shipment. It’s a statistical acceptance rule, not a defect cap.
linkedin

Paying 100% before inspection. You lose leverage if the goods fail.
redflagsolutions

No golden sample or defect list. Inspectors and suppliers interpret “defect” differently.
redflagsolutions

No epidemic‑failure or latent‑defect clause. You can pass AQL but still face high field return rates.

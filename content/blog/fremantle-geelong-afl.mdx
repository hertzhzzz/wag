## 2. The Purple Problem — Why Docker Purple Is the Hardest Colour in the AFL to Manufacture

Fremantle's purple is iconic. It is also a manufacturing nightmare.

### What Makes Purple Difficult

Purple is a secondary colour — a blend of red and blue pigments — and textile dyeing compounds the difficulty. Unlike navy blue (a single-pigment dye bath) or red (a straightforward primary), purple requires precise ratio control of red and blue dye components. A 3 per cent variance in the red-to-blue ratio shifts the colour from Docker purple to plum, violet, or burgundy. The human eye is exceptionally sensitive to purple hue shifts because purple sits at the edge of the visible spectrum, where small wavelength differences produce large perceptual differences.

In our experience vetting Chinese factories for AFL production, approximately 70 per cent of first-time Docker purple samples fail colour matching. The typical failure modes:

- **Too red** — The factory overcorrects for blue dominance in polyester dye uptake and produces a burgundy-adjacent shade that reads as maroon under stadium lighting
- **Too blue** — The factory uses a navy dye base and adds insufficient red, producing an indigo that Fremantle fans immediately identify as "not Docker purple"
- **Metameric failure** — The colour matches under factory fluorescent lighting but shifts visibly under natural daylight or Optus Stadium LED floodlights

### Production Specifications for Docker Purple

| Parameter | Required Specification | Common Factory Default |
|-----------|----------------------|-----------------------|
| Purple hue reference | Pantone 2685 C | Generic purple (no reference) |
| Red-to-blue dye ratio tolerance | plus-minus 2% | plus-minus 8-10% |
| Lightfastness | ISO 105-B02 Grade 4-plus | Grade 3 |
| Colour consistency across fabric types | Delta E less-than-or-equal-to 2.0 across polyester, acrylic, cotton twill | Delta E 5.0-8.0 |
| Metamerism index (D65 vs LED) | MI less-than 1.0 | MI 2.5-4.0 |

The Pantone 2685 C reference is critical. This is the specific purple used in Fremantle's official branding. A factory that produces "purple" without a Pantone reference will almost certainly miss the target. Equally important is the colour consistency requirement across fabric types. A purple that looks correct on a polyester guernsey may read as a different shade on an acrylic knit scarf or a cotton-twill cap because different fibre types accept dye differently.

The solution is a unified strike-off: require the factory to produce colour samples on all intended fabric substrates before approving bulk production. The $120 courier cost for a physical strike-off is insurance against a container of unsellable merchandise.

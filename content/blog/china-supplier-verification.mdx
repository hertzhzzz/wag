If you are importing from China for the first time, the single biggest risk is not product quality — it is paying a deposit to the wrong supplier.

This happens more often than most Australian businesses expect. A supplier looks legitimate online. They have a professional website, good photos, competitive pricing, and responsive communication. You negotiate for weeks. You pay a 30% deposit. Then production stalls, or the product that arrives is nothing like what was agreed, or the supplier goes quiet.

The problem is almost never that the supplier is a scammer. Most of the time, it is that the supplier is a trading company — an intermediary that connects buyers with factories — presenting itself as a direct manufacturer. They take your deposit, place your order with a factory they have a relationship with, and then manage the communication from the middle. When something goes wrong, there are two companies pointing at each other, and you are in the middle.

Verification prevents this. Not complicated verification — just a structured sequence of checks that tells you who you are actually dealing with, before you pay anything.

This guide covers what we have learned from 200+ factory visits across China. It is written for Australian businesses that are new to importing or that have had supplier problems and want a reliable process going forward.

## Why Australian Businesses Get Caught Out

The fundamental problem is that Chinese suppliers are optimised for export. Their online presence — Alibaba profile, company website, trade show booth — is built to attract international buyers. That presence is not a accurate picture of their capabilities. It is a sales tool.

For Australian businesses specifically, there are three patterns that cause the most problems.

**Trading companies posing as manufacturers.** A trading company sources from multiple factories. They can offer a wider product range and faster response times than most direct manufacturers. They present as one-stop shops. But when you pay a deposit, you are not dealing with the people who actually make your product.

**Exaggerated production capacity.** A factory that claims to have 200 workers and six production lines may have 40 workers and two lines. The photos on their website are real — they are just from a different time, or a partner facility. The only way to verify actual capacity is to see it in person or on a live video walkthrough.

**Quality control that exists on paper only.** Many suppliers have quality management certificates displayed in their showroom. They do not have quality control processes on the production floor. The certificate is real — it is just not describing what actually happens during production.

The common thread: these problems are invisible from Australia. You cannot verify a production floor from an email exchange. You cannot confirm machine count from a website. You cannot check business licence scope from a product catalog.

The solution is systematic verification before you pay a deposit. Not complicated. But sequential.

## What Verification Actually Protects You From

Most first-time importers think verification is about finding a "good" supplier. It is more precise than that.

Verification tells you two things:

**Who you are dealing with.** Manufacturer, trading company, or something in between. This matters because trading companies and manufacturers operate differently. A trading company takes a margin on every order. They do not control production. If something goes wrong, they have less ability to fix it than a direct factory would.

**What they can actually do.** Not what their website says they can do, not what their sales team claims — what the production floor shows on the day you visit or on a live video call. Production capacity, quality processes, machine condition, worker count. These are the things that determine whether your order arrives on time and to specification.

The deposit is the point of highest risk. Before you pay, you have maximum leverage — you can walk away. After you pay, your leverage drops significantly. Everything in this guide is about getting accurate information before you pay.

## Red Flags to Watch For Before You Travel

Verification starts before you book a flight. Some of the most useful signals come from your initial research.

**Their product catalog is unreasonably wide.** A supplier offering thirty different product categories across completely different industries — electronics, furniture, clothing, all in one catalog — is almost certainly aggregating from multiple factories. No single manufacturing operation covers that range well.

**They agree to every request immediately.** Experienced manufacturers know what is possible and what is not. A supplier that says yes to everything — custom specifications, tight timelines, unusual materials — is either not running their own production or is making promises they cannot keep.

**You cannot find their physical address independently.** A legitimate manufacturing operation has a registered address in an industrial zone. If the address they provide does not appear on satellite maps as an industrial facility, or if the address changes between your email and their website, ask before proceeding.

**They require a large deposit upfront.** Standard practice for first orders is 30% deposit, 70% balance against copy of Bill of Lading. If a supplier asks for 50% or more before any facility visit, treat that as a signal. Legitimate factories with export experience understand buyer concerns and are accustomed to payment structures that protect both parties.

**Their communication is only in sales mode.** A supplier that responds quickly and thoroughly to pricing questions but goes silent or gives vague answers when you ask about production processes, machine types, or quality control procedures is protecting their gaps. Real manufacturers have specific answers for technical questions because they run the production.

## Step 1: Pre-Trip Research (Do This Before You Travel)

If you are planning a sourcing trip to China, your verification starts at home. The checks below take a few hours and catch a significant proportion of problematic suppliers before you spend money on travel.

### Check the Business Licence Online

China's State Administration for Market Regulation maintains a public registry at samr.gov.cn. Every legitimate business has a registration record with a unified social credit code.

Search by the company's Chinese name or unified social credit code. What you are looking for:

- **Business scope**: Does it list manufacturing, production, or processing? If it only lists wholesale, export, or trade, you are dealing with a trading company. Manufacturing scope in the licence is the clearest signal that a company is a genuine factory.
- **Registered address**: Note this exactly. It should be in an industrial zone, not a commercial office building.
- **Company status**: Active, not in violation or abnormal status.
- **Registered capital**: Not a guarantee of anything, but unusually low registered capital for a business claiming large-scale operations is worth noting.

This takes about thirty minutes and is free. It will not tell you everything, but it will tell you whether you are dealing with a legitimate legal entity and whether manufacturing is in their scope.

### Verify Every Certificate Directly

If the supplier has provided quality certifications — ISO 9001, CE, CB, or anything else — verify each one directly with the issuing body.

Do not accept files or screenshots. Every legitimate certification body has an online verification portal. If the certificate number is real, it will verify. If it does not, you have found a problem.

What to check for each certificate:

- Does the certificate number exist in the issuing body's database?
- Does the company name match the company you are negotiating with exactly?
- Is the certificate current and within its validity period?
- Does the scope of certification cover the product you are ordering?

A certificate that is real does not necessarily mean the supplier is capable. It means they passed an audit at one point in time for specific products. Check the scope carefully.

### Use Satellite Maps to Verify the Address

Take the registered address from the business licence search and put it into Google Maps or Baidu Maps. Look at the satellite imagery.

A genuine factory will be in an industrial zone. You should see warehouse structures, loading dock areas, and possibly yard space. If the address resolves to a commercial office building or a residential area, that is a problem.

Compare the address the supplier uses on their website and in their email signature to the registered address. If they do not match, ask why before proceeding. Some suppliers list a marketing office in a commercial district and the actual factory elsewhere — this is not automatically a problem, but you need to know which one you are actually dealing with.

### Review Their Product Range Critically

Look at what they are offering and how they are offering it.

A manufacturer typically has a focused product range — they know their equipment, their processes, and their capabilities. A trading company aggregates across multiple factories and tends to have a wider, less coherent catalog.

Specific signs you may be dealing with a trading company:

- Products across three or more unrelated industry categories
- Minimum order quantities that are unusually low for the product type (trading companies are more flexible on MOQ because they are not running their own production)
- Pricing that is vague — no breakdown of tooling, packaging, or freight costs — because they are adding margin on top of factory pricing they do not fully control

These are signals, not verdicts. A supplier can have a wide range and still be a genuine manufacturer. But it is a reason to be more rigorous in your verification.

## Step 2: Request a Live Video Walkthrough

Before you commit to a travel budget, request a live video walkthrough of the production facility. Not a curated tour. Not pre-recorded footage. A live video call where you can see the production floor in real time and ask to be shown specific things.

A legitimate factory with real production capability will accommodate this. It is standard practice in international sourcing. If a supplier consistently deflects or offers to send photos instead, that itself is information — it means they do not have production capability they are willing to show you.

When you conduct the video walkthrough, focus on:

- **Are machines running?** A facility where all equipment is turned off during a scheduled video call is suspicious. Manufacturing equipment is expensive to sit idle.
- **Is the production relevant to your order?** If they claim to make your product, you should see something related running on the line during your call.
- **Does the scale match what they claimed?** Worker count, number of active lines, and facility size should be consistent with their capabilities.
- **Is the facility organised?** Clean, clearly labelled storage areas and visible QC stations indicate better management than chaotic or empty spaces.
- **Who are you talking to?** Ask to speak with the production manager, not just the sales team. A factory that separates sales from operations is a red flag in this context.

Ask to be shown the specific equipment that would handle your order. If they deflect or show generic footage instead of the actual production line, return to your pre-trip research and do more verification before proceeding.

## Step 3: On-Ground Factory Inspection

If you are making a sourcing trip to China — or if you have a significant order and want to verify in person — the factory visit is the most reliable verification step. Nothing replaces seeing the production floor with your own eyes.

The key principle: do not commit to any order on the day of the visit. Use the visit to gather information. Make your decision after you have compared what you saw against what was advertised.

### Before You Enter the Factory

Verify the registered address matches the facility you are visiting. Ask to see the business licence and confirm the company name, registered capital, and business scope. A legitimate factory will have this documentation available.

Ask to meet the production manager, not just the sales team. The production manager knows what the factory can actually do. Salespeople know what the factory wants to sell. These are not always the same thing.

### The Production Floor

Everything in the showroom is stage-managed. The production floor is where you see reality.

Walk the production floor systematically. What to look for:

- **Active production lines**: how many are running? Not in the brochure — today. If the factory claims to have six lines and you see two running, ask why.
- **Worker count**: does the number of workers on the floor match the facility size they claimed and the production volume they are quoting you?
- **Machine types and age**: do the machines match the product categories they claim to manufacture? A factory claiming to do precision injection moulding should have CNC machines and precision equipment, not just basic tooling.
- **QC checkpoints**: where in the production process does quality get checked? Quality control should be embedded throughout production, not just at final inspection.
- **Raw material storage**: are inputs stored cleanly and organised? Disorganised or empty storage areas suggest inconsistent material sourcing.
- **Output staging area**: what is waiting to be shipped? A full staging area suggests active orders. An empty staging area suggests they are not currently producing at the capacity they claim.

Ask specific questions while on the floor:

- "What is currently running on this line?"
- "How many workers are on shift today?"
- "When was this machine installed?"
- "Can you show me the QC records for last week's production?"

A factory that can show you what is planned versus what is running tells you about their operational management. A factory that cannot is either disorganised or showing you a staged version of their operation.

### Export Experience

For Australian businesses, export experience to developed markets matters. It means the supplier understands quality expectations, documentation requirements, and logistics processes that are different from domestic Chinese shipping.

Ask specifically:

- Which countries have they exported to? Ask for specific examples.
- Can they show documentation for their last three export shipments — commercial invoice, packing list, certificate of origin?
- Have they had a shipment rejected at customs, and what happened? Experienced exporters will give a specific answer. Inexperienced ones will say "we have no problems" — which is not a reassuring answer, because every exporter who has shipped internationally has had at least one customs question.
- Can they produce documentation that meets Australian customs requirements? This is a specific technical capability, not a general claim.

If the supplier cannot produce documentation for recent export shipments, that is a warning sign. Export documentation is a basic function of any business that regularly ships internationally.

### Subcontracting

Many factories — even legitimate ones — use subcontractors for specific processes. This is not inherently a problem, but you need to know about it.

Ask directly: "Do you use any third-party factories for this product?"

If they do, which processes are subcontracted? Can you visit the subcontractor's facility? How does the main factory quality-control the subcontractor's output?

A factory that claims to be a one-stop manufacturing facility but cannot explain their full production chain in detail is either ignorant of their own supply chain or is a trading company presenting as a manufacturer. Either way, you need to know before you pay a deposit.

## Step 4: Post-Visit Verification and Reference Checking

A factory visit is not the end of verification — it is the beginning of your decision-making process. After the visit, compare what you saw against what was advertised.

### Compare What You Saw Against the Claims

For every claim the supplier made — production capacity, worker count, export countries, certifications — check whether you saw evidence to support it during the visit.

Make notes on what you could verify and what you could not. The things you could not verify are the areas where you need more information before committing.

### Check References Directly

Ask the supplier for references from buyers in Australia, New Zealand, or other developed markets. Contact them directly.

Ask:

- What was quality consistency like across multiple orders?
- Did the supplier communicate responsively?
- Were deliveries on time and to specification?
- Would you work with this supplier again?

References from buyers in your target market tell you things that a factory visit cannot — specifically, what it is like to work with this supplier over multiple orders and across communication time zones.

### Request Samples

Before placing a production order, request samples from the top two candidates. This is not optional. A sample tells you whether the supplier can produce to the specification they quoted, not just whether they can talk about it.

Test the sample against your requirements. If the supplier cannot produce a sample that meets your specification, they will not produce a production order that meets it either.

### Never Rush the Decision

A week of comparing two factories is cheaper than a failed production order. First orders are the most vulnerable — the supplier is learning your requirements, and you are learning how this supplier operates. Give yourself time to make a considered decision.

## Step 5: Ongoing Due Diligence After Your First Order

Verification does not end when you place your first order. The first order is when your exposure is highest, because you have paid a deposit and the supplier knows your order is significant enough to merit attention.

For first orders above AUD 20,000, engage a third-party inspection service to conduct pre-shipment inspection. Companies like SGS, Bureau Veritas, or Asia Quality Focus inspect your goods before they leave China. The cost is typically 1-3% of the order value — a fraction of what a rejected shipment costs you.

What pre-shipment inspection catches that verification does not:

- Production quality against your specification
- Quantity accuracy
- Packaging and labelling compliance
- Any issues that arose during production that the supplier did not communicate

For higher-value or more complex orders, consider mid-production inspection — checking in during production, not just at the end. Problems caught during production can be corrected. Problems caught at pre-shipment inspection require negotiation.

## The Decision Framework

Work through these steps in sequence. Each step catches something that the previous step cannot.

1. **Business licence check** (Step 1): free, 30 minutes. Catches trading companies and shell companies. Do this before you spend significant time negotiating.

2. **Certificate and address verification** (Step 1): free, 2 hours. Catches expired certificates and mismatched addresses. Do this before requesting samples or paying any deposit.

3. **Live video walkthrough** (Step 2): free if the supplier arranges it, 1-2 weeks to schedule. Catches facilities that are smaller or less capable than advertised. Do this before you book travel.

4. **On-ground factory visit** (Step 3): travel cost, 1-2 days on-site. The most reliable verification step. Do this for orders above AUD 10,000 or where you have any uncertainty after Steps 1-2.

5. **Reference checking** (Step 4): free, 2-3 hours. Tells you what it is like to work with this supplier over time. Do this before you commit to a first order.

6. **Pre-shipment inspection** (Step 5): AUD 300-500 for standard orders. Catches production problems before your goods leave China. Do this for all orders above AUD 20,000.

## Red Flags Summary

These signals, individually, may have explanations. Two or more together should cause you to pause before committing:

| Red Flag | What It May Signal |
|----------|-------------------|
| Refuses to show the production floor on video | Trading company or subcontracts everything |
| Fast agreement to every request | Over-promising; inability to deliver |
| No export experience to Australia or similar markets | Unfamiliarity with quality expectations |
| Cannot show documentation for recent export shipments | New to exporting or misrepresenting volume |
| Salespeople who cannot introduce production manager | Disconnect between sales and operations |
| Pushes for large deposit before any facility visit | Potential scam risk |
| Registered address is a commercial building, not industrial zone | Trading company or shell operation |
| Product catalog covers three or more unrelated categories | Aggregation from multiple factories |

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why is supplier verification important for Australian businesses importing from China?

Australian businesses are particularly vulnerable to supplier verification problems because of the distance involved. You cannot easily visit a factory to check what is happening. By the time you discover a problem, you have already paid a deposit and the supplier has your money. Verification catches trading companies posing as manufacturers, exaggerated production capacity, and quality control gaps before you pay anything.

### What is the first step in verifying a Chinese supplier?

The first step is checking the business licence on samr.gov.cn — China's State Administration for Market Regulation. This confirms whether the company is a legitimate legal entity and whether manufacturing is in their registered business scope. If the business scope only lists trade or wholesale, you are dealing with a trading company, not a manufacturer.

### How do I know if a Chinese supplier is a real manufacturer or a trading company?

A trading company will have "wholesale" or "export" in their business licence scope, not "manufacturing" or "production." They typically offer a wider product range than a single factory could produce, have unusually low minimum order quantities, and cannot show you their own production floor on a live video call. Ask to see their production floor during a live video walkthrough — a real factory will accommodate this.

### What questions should I ask during a factory visit in China?

Ask about current production volume and what is running on the production line today. Ask to meet the production manager, not just the sales team. Ask to see QC records from recent production runs. Ask specifically about their experience exporting to Australia — have they had any shipments rejected at customs, and what happened? Request documentation for their last three export shipments. If they cannot produce this documentation, that is a warning sign.

### How do I verify a Chinese supplier's export experience to Australia?

Ask for documentation from recent Australian shipments — commercial invoices, packing lists, certificate of origin. Call the references they provide. Ask whether they understand Australian import requirements including any relevant certifications for your product category. A supplier with genuine Australian export experience will have specific answers to these questions, not general reassurance.

### Should I visit factories in Shenzhen or Guangzhou?

Both cities are manufacturing hubs, but they serve different roles. Shenzhen has a higher concentration of sophisticated manufacturers with extensive export experience — more suitable for precision products and complex assemblies. Guangzhou factories tend to be more cost-competitive for larger volume orders in categories like furniture, textiles, and basic consumer goods. For your first sourcing trip, visiting factories in both cities gives you a better picture of what is available.

### What is a factory audit and do I need one?

A factory audit is a structured inspection of a manufacturing facility to verify its capabilities, quality systems, and compliance. For orders above AUD 30,000, an independent on-site audit is worth the cost — typically AUD 800-2,000 depending on complexity. For smaller orders, the video walkthrough and on-ground visit steps in this guide catch most problems. Audit reports from third-party agencies are useful but represent a point-in-time snapshot — they do not replace your own verification.

### How many factories should I visit before making a decision?

Visit at least two factories for the same product category before committing. Comparing suppliers side by side gives you benchmarks that a single visit cannot provide. You will see pricing, quality, and communication patterns in context. One factory's quote means nothing without another to compare it against. A week of comparison is cheaper than a failed order.

### What is the biggest red flag when dealing with a Chinese supplier for the first time?

Refusal to show the production floor on a live video call is the most serious warning sign. Legitimate factories that export internationally accommodate buyer verification routinely — it is standard practice. If a supplier insists on showing only the showroom or conference room, they may not own the production facilities they claim to have. This single red flag justifies returning to earlier verification steps before proceeding.

### How do I check a Chinese supplier's business licence remotely?

Search the company on samr.gov.cn using their Chinese name or unified social credit code. Check the business scope for manufacturing, production, or processing terms. Verify the registered address — it should be in an industrial zone, not a commercial office building. Check the company status for any abnormal business notices. This is free and takes about thirty minutes. It will not tell you everything, but it will tell you whether manufacturing is in their scope at all.

### What inspection services do you recommend for Australian buyers importing from China?

SGS, Bureau Veritas, and Asia Quality Focus are the most widely used third-party inspection services. Pre-shipment inspections typically cost AUD 300-500 for standard orders and cover quantity, packaging, and quality against specification. For complex orders or orders above AUD 30,000, mid-production inspections are worth the additional cost — they catch problems during production rather than after it is complete.

### What is the minimum verification I should do before paying a deposit?

At minimum: check the business licence on samr.gov.cn, verify any certificates directly with the issuing body, confirm the registered address on satellite maps, and request a live video walkthrough of the production floor. These four steps take under three hours combined and catch the most common problems — trading companies, expired certificates, and mismatched addresses. If anything in these steps is unclear, do not proceed.

### How do I protect myself when paying a deposit to a Chinese supplier?

Use payment terms that protect you: 30% deposit, 70% balance against copy of Bill of Lading. Never pay 50% or more upfront before seeing the facility. Confirm the business bank account is in the same company name as the manufacturing licence. Use a supplier with documented export experience — they understand the documentation that protects both parties in international trade.

### What is the difference between supplier verification and product inspection?

Verification tells you whether you are dealing with a legitimate, capable supplier before you place an order. Inspection tells you whether the product you ordered meets your specification at the time of shipping. Both are necessary. Verification prevents you from paying a deposit to the wrong supplier. Inspection prevents you from receiving the wrong product. Use both — verification before you pay, inspection before you accept delivery.

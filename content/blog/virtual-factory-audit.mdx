Not every Australian business can travel to China for every supplier. Whether due to cost, time, or global events, remote supplier verification is a legitimate tool — as long as you understand its limitations.

This guide covers how to conduct a virtual factory audit, which tools to use, and what a remote inspection can and cannot verify.


## What a Virtual Audit Can and Cannot Do

### Can Do

- Verify the legal entity exists and matches the claim
- Confirm production scale through facility video
- Observe active production and working conditions
- Verify business licence and basic credentials
- Conduct interviews with management
- Review documentation and certifications

### Cannot Do

- Verify machine condition through video
- Confirm precise production tolerances
- Smell chemical or finish issues
- Physically test product samples
- Assess the full production environment in detail
- Replace physical inspection for critical orders

<Tip>
Use virtual audits for initial screening and ongoing monitoring. For first orders above a threshold that matters to your business, plan an in-person visit before committing significant volume or capital.
</Tip>

## Step 1: Business Licence Verification

Before any video call, verify the legal entity. This takes minutes and catches the most common fraud.


**How to verify:**
Use a Chinese business credit agency. The two main services are:
- **Qichacha (企查查)** — [qcc.com](https://www.qcc.com) (in Chinese, requires Chinese language)
- **Tianyancha (天眼查)** — [tianyancha.com](https://www.tianyancha.com)

Both provide:
- Registered company name and unified social credit code
- Registered capital
- Business scope (should include manufacturing if claiming to manufacture)
- Legal representative
- Shareholder structure
- Any outstanding legal judgments

**What to look for:**
- The registered name matches what they are presenting
- Business scope includes manufacturing (not just trading)
- Registered capital is consistent with their claimed scale
- No outstanding legal issues

## Step 2: Third-Party Audit Reports

Request existing audit reports from services your supplier may have undergone:

- **[Sedex](https://www.sedex.com)** — ethical trade audit
- **[BSCI](https://www.amfori.org)** — business social compliance initiative
- **SA8000** — social accountability
- **[ISO 9001](https://www.iso.org)** — quality management

These reports represent a point-in-time inspection. Ask:
- When was the audit conducted?
- What was the result?
- Were any non-conformances identified?
- Has the factory addressed the non-conformances?

<Tip>
A third-party audit report is useful but not sufficient on its own. It represents one moment in time and may not cover the specific processes relevant to your product.
</Tip>

## Step 3: Live Video Inspection

If the factory passes initial screening, arrange a live video inspection.


**How to arrange:**
- Request a video call with the person who would manage your account
- Ask them to show you the production floor via their phone or tablet
- Have a bilingual guide or interpreter on the call to ensure clear communication

**What to observe on video:**
- Does the facility look like a manufacturing operation?
- Are there workers on the production floor?
- Are machines the type consistent with the products claimed?
- Is the facility the same address as on the business licence?
- Do the staff seem organized and professional?

### Video Inspection Checklist

| Check | What to Look For |
|-------|-----------------|
| Production floor | Active production, workers present, appropriate equipment |
| Warehouse | Raw material storage, finished goods waiting to ship |
| Office | Professional setup, documentation visible |
| Staff | Do they seem familiar with the operation? |
| Address | Does the location match the business licence? |
| Scale | Does the facility match the capacity they claimed? |

## Step 4: Documentation Review

Request the following documentation and review via email or video call:

- Business licence (with English translation if available)
- Export registration certificate
- Quality management system certification (ISO 9001 or equivalent)
- Product-specific certifications relevant to your market
- Test reports for products similar to what you are sourcing
- References from other buyers (especially in your market or product category)

## Step 5: Sample Testing

A virtual audit must be combined with physical sample verification:


- Order samples before placing any production order
- Test samples against your specifications
- Use an independent testing laboratory for critical parameters
- Australian testing labs can verify compliance with Australian standards

**For samples from China:**
Use a consolidation service that can forward samples to your Australian address, or use a local testing agent in China who can receive samples and arrange Australian-standard testing.

## Limitations to Acknowledge

Virtual audits have genuine limitations you should acknowledge to your team and stakeholders:

1. **Machine condition** — you cannot verify machine calibration or maintenance status through video
2. **Product quality** — samples sent are typically the best the factory can produce; production quality may differ
3. **Worker treatment** — video inspections can be staged; look for signs of staged environments
4. **Process control** — you cannot verify in-process quality controls that happen between production stages
5. **Storage and handling** — conditions in the warehouse may not reflect actual shipping conditions

<Tip>
For critical purchases, use virtual audits as the screening step, not the final step. A virtual audit that identifies a supplier as credible should still be followed by an in-person visit before committing significant volume.
</Tip>

## When Virtual Is Not Enough

Do not rely solely on virtual verification for:

- First orders above AUD 20,000-30,000
- Products where quality is difficult to verify through samples alone
- Suppliers identified through online directories with no trade show or referral history
- Products with specific safety or regulatory requirements

In these cases, plan a physical visit or engage a third-party inspection company with on-ground presence.

## Video Call Protocols: Maximising Audit Value

The quality of a virtual factory audit depends heavily on how the video call is conducted. A poorly structured call misses information that a well-structured call would capture.

**Before the call:** Send a structured agenda to the supplier that includes the areas you will inspect: production floor, warehouse, quality control area, office, and any specific equipment relevant to your product. This gives the factory time to prepare relevant documentation and ensures key staff are available.

**During the call:** Use a consistent sequence — start at the entrance of the facility and work through systematically, narrating what you are looking for so the guide knows what to show. Photograph or screen-record everything. Ask for close-ups of equipment nameplates, certification documents, and production line details.

**Red flags during video:** If the video feed is consistently blurry, if the guide rushes past areas quickly, if they claim equipment is not operational that day, or if they redirect you to a different facility without explanation, treat these as serious concerns. Request specific footage of the items that raised questions before ending the call.

**Follow-up requests:** After the call, request the specific footage you did not get or clarification on areas that were unclear. A legitimate factory will provide this. A deceptive supplier will make excuses.

## Digital Due Diligence Tools for China Verification

Beyond video calls, several digital tools support remote factory verification:

**Satellite imagery.** Google Maps and Baidu Maps provide satellite imagery of factory addresses. Confirm the address corresponds to an industrial facility with appropriate scale. Compare imagery timestamps when available — facilities that appear in older imagery provide additional confidence.

**Reverse image search.** Use Google reverse image search on factory photographs to identify whether images are copied from other websites or appear in multiple contexts. Deceptive suppliers sometimes use stock photos or images from legitimate factories to represent their own operations.

**Trade data verification.** Platforms like ImportGenius and Panjiva provide trade transaction data that can verify a company's export experience. A factory claiming to export to Australia should have corresponding customs records. This data is not definitive but provides supporting evidence.

**Social media and news verification.** Search for the company name in Chinese news databases and on business social networks. Companies with active operations typically appear in industry news, trade exhibition listings, and business networking profiles. Absence of any online presence for a company claiming significant operations is a red flag.

**Web archive review.** Use the Wayback Machine to review how a supplier's website has evolved over time. A website that was created recently, or that has inconsistent history, may indicate a company that is not what it claims. Legitimate factories typically have longer web presence histories.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How reliable are Chinese business licence databases?

Reasonably reliable for basic verification. The Chinese government maintains these databases, and companies have legal obligations to report accurate information. However, the databases do not capture operational quality, production capability, or ongoing compliance. Use them as a starting point, not a final verdict.

### Can a virtual audit replace an in-person visit?

No — not for significant purchasing decisions. A virtual audit catches major red flags and gives you a basic level of confidence, but it cannot replace walking the production floor, touching the equipment, and seeing the operation in person. For critical orders, plan a visit.

### What does a third-party inspection company cost?

Third-party inspection in China typically ranges from AUD 300-800 per day depending on the complexity and the inspection company. For a full factory audit with a written report, expect AUD 500-1,500 per factory. This is a minor cost relative to the order value it is protecting.

### How do I find a reliable third-party inspection company?

Look for companies with specific experience in your product category and your target market. In Australia, companies like [SGS](https://www.sgs.com), [Bureau Veritas](https://www.bureauveritas.com), and Intertek have Chinese operations. In China directly, firms like [QIMA](https://www.qima.com) and Asia Quality Focus specialize in supplier audits for international buyers. For a full verification checklist covering all the steps in this guide, see our [supplier verification checklist for Chinese factories](/resources/supplier-verification-checklist-china).

### Can I verify a Chinese business license without speaking Chinese?

Yes. Use Tianyancha or Qichacha with the company name or unified social credit code. Both platforms have some English-language interface. A bilingual guide can help interpret the business scope section which is critical for identifying trading companies versus manufacturers.

### What is the minimum verification I should do before paying a deposit?

At minimum: check the business license through a Chinese credit agency, verify the registered address with satellite imagery, and request a live video walkthrough of the production line. If any of these raise red flags, do not proceed until you have answers.

### How do I verify certifications without speaking Chinese?

Use the certification body website directly — search for the certifier by name (Bureau Veritas, SGS, TUV, etc.) and use their online certificate verification tool. Most major certifiers have English-language verification portals. Do not use links provided by the supplier.

### Should I rely on virtual audits for all suppliers?

No. Virtual audits are appropriate for initial screening and ongoing monitoring of existing suppliers. For new suppliers, first orders above AUD 20,000-30,000, or products with specific safety requirements, plan an in-person visit before committing significant volume.

### Can virtual audits replace physical factory visits entirely?

No. Virtual audits catch major red flags and provide basic confidence but cannot replace walking the production floor, touching equipment, or seeing operations in person. For critical orders, virtual audits should be followed by physical visits.

### What video call platform should I use for virtual factory audits?

WeChat video is the most reliable option as most Chinese factories use WeChat for communications. If the factory uses WeChat, request a video call through that platform. Alternative platforms like Zoom or Google Meet may be difficult for Chinese factories to access due to internet restrictions. Ensure you have a functional WeChat account with video calling capability before the audit.

### How do I handle a supplier that refuses live video inspection?

Refusal to conduct live video inspection is a serious red flag. Legitimate factories with genuine manufacturing operations have no reason to refuse live video of their facility. If a supplier refuses, disqualify them and move to the next candidate on your shortlist. Do not accept recorded videos or photographs in place of live inspection as these can be staged or archived from other facilities.

### What should I photograph during a virtual factory audit?

Photograph everything visible on the video call: equipment nameplates showing model numbers and manufacturers, certification documents on walls, production floor layout, worker ID badges (blurred if needed for privacy), warehouse contents and labelling, quality control area and testing equipment, loading dock area, and any discrepancies between what the supplier claimed and what the video shows. Screen recording is also valuable for documentation.

### How do I verify a factory's export experience remotely?

Request copies of export documentation — packing lists, customs declarations, or commercial invoices that show shipment details. These documents contain company name, product descriptions, quantity, and destination. Cross-reference these with the factory's claimed export markets. You can also use trade data platforms like ImportGenius or Panjiva to verify export records exist for the markets the factory claims to serve.

Before an Australian business pays a deposit to a Chinese supplier, there is one question that matters more than price: **are the certifications real?**

A factory in Guangdong can print an ISO 9001 certificate on glossy paper and frame it in the showroom. That does not mean the certificate is valid, current, or even belongs to that factory. Through our work with 1,311 Chinese factories holding 1,758 certifications, we have seen every variation of this problem: expired certificates, certificates registered to a different company, and certificates from issuing bodies that do not exist.

This guide covers the six certifications Australian importers encounter most often, how to verify each one, and the four red flags that should make you stop before wiring a deposit.

## The Six Certifications That Matter for Australian Importers

Across the 1,311 factories in our database, six certifications dominate. Here is what each one actually means — and what it does not mean.

### ISO 9001 — Quality Management Systems

154 factories in our database hold ISO 9001 certification, with the highest concentration in Guangdong (90, including an [audited manufacturer](/factory/aosua)) and Zhejiang (25). Among super factories that have passed deep on-site audits, 25 hold ISO 9001.

**What it means:** The factory has a documented quality management system. It does not mean the factory produces high-quality products — it means there is a system for managing quality, and an auditor has verified that system exists.

**How to verify:** Every legitimate ISO 9001 certificate has a unique number. Enter that number at the IAF CertSearch database (iafcertsearch.org), the global registry of accredited management system certifications. If the certificate number returns no result, or the company name on the certificate does not match the IAF record, the certificate is not valid.

**Red flag:** A supplier who will not share the certificate number. A glossy scan of a certificate without the number visible is worthless.

### TUV — Product Safety and Conformity

TUV is the most common certification in our database: 457 factories hold some form of TUV certification, including 61 super factories. Guangdong leads with 222, followed by Zhejiang with 100, and Jiangsu with 27. Many of these are super factories that have passed additional on-site [FCA audits](/factory/aimagic).

**What it means:** TUV Rheinland (Germany) and TUV SUD are independent testing organisations. Their certifications cover product safety, electromagnetic compatibility, and conformity to European standards. For Australian importers, a TUV certificate is a strong indicator that the product has been independently tested to international safety standards.

**How to verify:** Go to TUV Rheinland's certificate database (certipedia.com) or TUV SUD's certificate checker. Enter the certificate ID. The database shows the certificate holder's name, the product scope, and the expiry date. If the certificate is not in the database, it was either never issued or has been withdrawn.

### SGS — Inspection and Testing

440 factories hold SGS certification, including 60 super factories. Guangdong has 184, Zhejiang 116.

**What it means:** SGS (Societe Generale de Surveillance, Switzerland) is one of the world's largest inspection and testing companies. An SGS certificate typically means the factory's products have passed laboratory testing for specific standards — often related to chemical composition, mechanical properties, or food-contact safety.

**How to verify:** SGS maintains an online certified clients directory. Enter the certificate number and the system returns the certification scope, issue date, and validity period. Cross-check the company name on the certificate with the name on the business licence.

### Intertek — Testing and Compliance

366 factories hold Intertek certification, including 61 super factories. Guangdong dominates with 151, followed by Zhejiang with 101. See a [verified factory from this region](/factory/albbthj).

**What it means:** Intertek (UK) provides testing, inspection, and certification services. Their factory certifications typically cover product testing against international standards — electrical safety (IEC), toy safety (EN 71), textile quality, and food safety.

**How to verify:** Intertek's Certificate Verification Portal accepts certificate numbers and returns the certification status, scope, and validity. If the certificate claims to cover a product category that Intertek does not test, the certificate is fabricated.

### CE Marking — European Market Access

174 factories in the database hold CE certification, with 108 in Guangdong and 33 in Zhejiang. Twenty super factories have CE certification.

**What it means for Australian importers:** CE marking is required for products sold in the European Economic Area. While Australia has its own regulatory framework (RCM), many Australian importers treat CE certification as a useful quality signal — if a product meets EU standards, it is likely close to meeting Australian standards. However, CE marking is self-declared for many product categories, meaning the manufacturer — not an independent body — declares conformity.

**How to verify:** For product categories requiring third-party assessment (medical devices, pressure equipment, some construction products), ask for the notified body's certificate number and verify it with that body. For self-declared categories, ask for the Declaration of Conformity and the supporting test reports from an accredited laboratory.

**Red flag:** A CE certificate for a self-declared product category issued by an "independent certification body." That body may not be a genuine EU-notified body. Always verify the notified body's four-digit NB number at the EU NANDO database.

### CCC — China Compulsory Certification

59 factories hold CCC certification, concentrated in Guangdong (32) and Zhejiang (18). Thirty-two super factories hold CCC.

**What it means:** CCC is mandatory for certain product categories sold within China. For Australian importers, CCC is relevant because it tells you the factory has been audited by Chinese authorities. However, CCC does not guarantee the product meets Australian standards — it only confirms compliance with Chinese regulations for the Chinese market.

**How to verify:** The Certification and Accreditation Administration of China (CNCA) maintains an online CCC certificate query system. Enter the certificate number and the system shows the certificate holder, product category, and validity period.

## How to Verify a Certificate in 3 Steps

Every certification body provides an online verification portal. Here is the process.

**Step 1: Get the certificate number.** Ask the supplier for a copy of the certificate showing the full certificate number. If the number is blurred, redacted, or "unavailable," stop. A real certification body wants their certificates to be verifiable.

**Step 2: Verify through the issuing body.** Go directly to the certification body's website — not a link the supplier sends you. Enter the certificate number. Check that the certificate holder's name matches the business licence name exactly (Chinese name and English name, if applicable).

**Step 3: Check the scope and expiry.** The certificate has a defined scope — specific product categories, specific standards. If you are ordering LED lighting and the supplier's ISO 9001 scope covers "textile manufacturing," the certificate does not cover your order. Check the expiry date: ISO 9001 certificates are valid for three years and require annual surveillance audits.

## Four Red Flags That Should Make You Stop

**1. The certificate number cannot be found in the issuing body's database.** This is the most common issue. The certificate is either expired, withdrawn, or never existed. A supplier who says "the database is down" or "it takes time to update" is making excuses.

**2. The company name on the certificate does not match the business licence.** Some factories show certificates registered to a different legal entity — a subsidiary, a related company, or a former name. This is not necessarily fraud, but it means the certification does not apply to the factory you are dealing with.

**3. The certification scope excludes your product category.** A factory may hold ISO 9001 for "plastic injection moulding" but be producing metal stampings under the same certificate. That certification is not valid for your order.

**4. The certificate is expired.** ISO 9001 certificates have a three-year validity with annual surveillance. A certificate from 2022 with no surveillance audit records should be treated as expired. Ask for the most recent surveillance audit report.

## Australian Import Compliance: Certification and RCM

For Australian importers, the key regulatory framework is the Regulatory Compliance Mark (RCM). RCM is required for electrical and electronic products, and it is the manufacturer's or importer's responsibility to ensure compliance.

A CE certificate is not automatically valid for RCM. However, if the product has been tested to IEC standards by an accredited laboratory (which CE often requires), those test reports can support RCM compliance. Ask your supplier for the underlying IEC test reports — not just the CE certificate.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I trust a supplier who only has CCC certification?

CCC confirms the factory meets Chinese regulatory requirements for products sold in China. It does not confirm that the product meets Australian standards. For Australian importers, CCC alone is not sufficient — look for ISO 9001 or an international certification like TUV, SGS, or Intertek. If the factory only has CCC, ask why they have not pursued international certifications. The answer may reveal whether they have ever exported to Western markets.

### How often should I re-verify certifications?

Every time you place a new order, or at minimum every 12 months. Certifications expire, surveillance audits can be failed, and factories can lose certifications between orders. A valid certificate from your last order does not guarantee validity today.

### What if the supplier refuses to share the certificate number?

Walk away. A legitimate factory with real certifications wants buyers to verify them — it is a competitive advantage. A supplier who hides certificate numbers is either showing fake documents or knows the certificates do not belong to them.

### Do Australian importers need to care about certifications at all if they visit the factory in person?

Yes. A factory visit reveals the production floor, equipment, and working conditions. It does not reveal whether the factory's quality management system meets ISO 9001 standards, or whether their products have passed independent safety testing. Certification verification and factory visits complement each other — one checks the paperwork, the other checks the reality.

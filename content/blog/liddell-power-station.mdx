The closure of Liddell Power Station marks one of the most consequential energy infrastructure events in Australia's recent history. For Australian businesses that rely on energy equipment sourced from China — or that are considering pivoting their supply chains toward Chinese manufacturers — the transition carries distinct strategic implications that deserve careful analysis.

This article examines what the Liddell closure means for Australia's energy supply landscape, how Chinese manufacturers are positioning themselves to serve the Australian market's evolving needs, and what it means for your business if you're importing energy-related equipment from China.

## What Was Liddell Power Station and Why Did It Close?

Liddell Power Station was a coal-fired thermal power plant located in the New South Wales Hunter Valley region, operated by AGL Energy. With a generating capacity of 2,000 megawatts, it was one of the largest coal-fired power stations in Australia's operational fleet.

The station was originally commissioned in 1971 and had originally been scheduled for closure around 2022. AGL subsequently brought forward the closure date to 2023 following an agreement with the Australian government, in exchange for allowing the merger between Origin Energy and Acacia Coal — a deal that ultimately did not proceed. The plant officially ceased operations in April 2023, marking the end of nearly five decades of coal-fired electricity generation.

The closure was driven by several converging factors:

- **Age and inefficiency**: The plant's design made it increasingly costly to maintain relative to newer generation technologies
- **Government pressure**: Federal and state governments pushed for earlier closure as part of national emissions reduction commitments
- **Economic viability**: Renewables had reached price parity or better in many Australian markets, making older thermal plants less competitive
- **Reputation and investor sentiment**: Major institutional investors had been progressively divesting from coal assets, increasing financing costs for operators

## How the Closure Affects Australia's Energy Supply Chain

The departure of Liddell from the national electricity grid has created a measurable gap in baseload generation capacity. While the official narrative has emphasised the role of renewables — particularly solar and wind — in filling this void, the reality is more nuanced for businesses that depend on consistent, cost-effective power supply.

### Baseload vs Intermittent Supply

Coal plants like Liddell provided what is known as "baseload" power — electricity generated continuously at a relatively stable rate regardless of weather conditions. Solar and wind, by contrast, are intermittent: they generate only when the sun shines or the wind blows.

| Energy Source | Baseload Capability | Intermittency Risk | Typical Cost Trend |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coal (Liddell type) | High | Low | Declining as plants age |
| Natural Gas | High | Low | Volatile (linked to fuel prices) |
| Solar PV | Low | High (night + cloud cover) | Falling rapidly |
| Wind | Low | High (variable wind patterns) | Falling rapidly |
| Battery Storage | Medium | Low (but duration limited) | Falling but still significant |

The result is that while the aggregate megawatt-hours generated from renewables has increased substantially, businesses in certain regions — particularly in NSW and Queensland — have faced elevated spot prices during transition periods, especially in summer peaks when demand is highest and solar generation tails off in late afternoon.

### Transmission Infrastructure Constraints

The Hunter Valley region where Liddell stood is also the junction point for significant transmission infrastructure connecting coal-heavy generation in the west to demand centres along the east coast. The closure accelerates pressure on transmission operators — Ausgrid, Transgrid, and ElectraNet — to upgrade and reconfigure the grid to accommodate distributed renewable generation from places like the Darling Downs, Broken Hill, and the Murray Region.

For businesses, this means grid stability in some areas will remain a concern through at least 2027–2028 as these upgrades are completed.

## China's Energy Equipment Manufacturers and the Australian Opportunity

If there is a beneficiary of the Liddell closure and Australia's broader energy transition, it is arguably the Chinese energy equipment manufacturing sector. Chinese manufacturers have been investing aggressively in renewable energy technology for over a decade, and their products now span the full spectrum of energy equipment relevant to Australia's transition.

### Solar Modules and Inverters

Chinese solar manufacturers — particularly companies like LONGi, Trina Solar, JinkoSolar, and Canadian Solar — dominate global solar module production. These companies have extensive experience supplying the Australian market and have built distribution networks specifically for Australian conditions.

Australian businesses importing solar equipment from China should be aware of:

- **Quality certification requirements**: Solar modules and inverters must meet Australian standards, primarily AS/NZS 4777 (for inverters) and the Clean Energy Regulator's requirements for CEC listing
- **Warranty considerations**: Many Chinese manufacturers offer 25–30 year linear power output warranties on modules, but warranty claims require a local representative or distributor
- **Supply chain lead times**: Module lead times typically run 8–14 weeks from order confirmation; inverter lead times can be 12–20 weeks for larger units

### Battery Storage Systems

China is also the dominant manufacturer of lithium iron phosphate (LFP) battery cells, which are increasingly preferred for large-scale and commercial energy storage applications due to their thermal stability and long cycle life. Chinese manufacturers such as CATL, BYD, and EVE Energy produce battery storage systems that are being adopted across Australian commercial and industrial installations.

Key considerations for businesses sourcing battery storage from China:

- **Integration complexity**: Battery systems require careful integration with inverters and management systems; not all Chinese cells are compatible with all inverter brands
- **Shipping and customs**: Lithium batteries are classified as dangerous goods and face specific shipping regulations and customs requirements in Australia
- **Installation standards**: Battery installations must comply with AS/NZS 5139 (Electrical installations — Safety of battery systems for stationary applications), and installations above certain capacity thresholds require certified electricians

### Wind Turbine Components

While wind turbine manufacturing is more concentrated among a handful of global players (Vestas, Siemens Gamesa, GE Renewable Energy), Chinese manufacturers such as CSSC and MingYang supply components and increasingly complete turbine systems into international markets. Australian wind farm developers have begun exploring Chinese turbine suppliers as an alternative to the traditional European supply chain, particularly for smaller or community-scale projects.

### Transmission and Distribution Equipment

The grid upgrade process in Australia requires substantial quantities of transformer equipment, switchgear, cables, and monitoring systems. Chinese manufacturers — including XD Group, TBEA, and NARI Technology — are established global suppliers in this space. Australian utilities and industrial buyers have been sourcing this type of equipment from China for years, and the Liddell closure-related transmission upgrades have increased inquiry levels.

## Strategic Implications for Australian Businesses

The energy transition triggered by the Liddell closure creates both risks and opportunities for Australian businesses. Here is what you should be thinking about.

### Rising Energy Costs and Contract Structures

The closure has contributed to upward pressure on electricity prices in the National Electricity Market (NEM), particularly in NSW and Queensland. Businesses that have historically purchased electricity on fixed-price contracts may find renewal rates significantly higher. Companies that self-generate — for example, via solar + battery systems — have a much clearer path to cost predictability.

**Action point**: Review your electricity contracts now. If your current contract is expiring within 12 months, begin negotiating or exploring self-generation options. The economics of solar-plus-storage have improved substantially, and Chinese-manufactured systems now represent a cost-competitive option for businesses with suitable roof or land space.

### Supply Chain Diversification and Resilience

The Liddell closure is not an isolated event — it is part of a broader global energy transition that is creating supply chain pressures in multiple directions simultaneously. Demand for solar modules, batteries, and grid equipment is surging globally as country after country accelerates its renewable buildout. Chinese manufacturers are running at high capacity utilisation, and lead times have lengthened for several product categories.

**Action point**: Do not wait until you need equipment to order it. If your business is considering solar, battery, or grid upgrade projects, initiate supplier engagement now. The typical procurement cycle for Chinese-sourced energy equipment — from initial enquiry to delivery and installation — runs 4–9 months depending on product type and customisation requirements.

### Quality vs Price Trade-offs

The Australian market's experience with solar modules has included some cautionary tales — particularly around early-generation products from manufacturers that subsequently exited the market, leaving customers with warranty claims they could not enforce. As the market for battery storage and grid equipment grows, the same risk profile applies.

**Action point**: When evaluating Chinese energy equipment suppliers, verify the manufacturer's track record in the Australian market specifically. Check whether they have a local representative or distributor who can honour warranty claims. Review the financial stability of the manufacturer — particularly for batteries, where after-sales support and RMA (return merchandise authorisation) processes are critical.

### Opportunities in Second-Life and Repurposing

One emerging opportunity that relates indirectly to the Liddell closure is the secondary market for repowering and repurposing. As older coal sites like Liddell are decommissioned, the land and grid connection points become available for renewable energy installations. Australian businesses involved in energy project development, property development, or industrial operations may find opportunities to participate in these repurposing projects.

Chinese EPC (Engineering, Procurement, and Construction) firms with renewable experience are actively seeking partnerships in Australian market. Businesses with suitable land or infrastructure access may be well positioned to act as local partners for Chinese firms bringing capital and equipment to Australian projects.

## How Australian Businesses Are Currently Using China Supply for Energy

Based on our engagement with Australian businesses across the energy sector, several patterns are emerging.

**Direct import of solar and battery equipment**: A growing number of commercial and industrial businesses are importing solar modules and battery systems directly from Chinese manufacturers, either working with Australian-based trading companies or engaging Chinese exporters directly. This approach offers the best pricing but requires stronger internal procurement and technical capability.

**Project-based procurement through Australian distributors**: Businesses without dedicated procurement teams often prefer to purchase through Australian distributors who import, warehouse, and provide local support. Distributors typically charge a 10–20% premium over direct importation but handle compliance, logistics, and after-sales support.

**Turnkey EPC contracts**: For businesses building larger installations (over 100kW for solar or 500kWh for battery), Chinese EPC firms are increasingly offering turnkey contracts that include design, supply, installation, and commissioning. Australian businesses should verify that these firms hold appropriate electrical licences and contractor registrations in the relevant state.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long did it take for energy prices to stabilise after Liddell closed?

The short answer is that prices have not fully stabilised. In the 24 months following the April 2023 closure, average spot prices in the NSW region remained elevated relative to pre-closure levels, though they were also influenced by other factors including gas price volatility and increased renewable penetration. Businesses should plan for continued price variability through at least 2026–2027 as new transmission infrastructure comes online.

### Can I still import coal equipment from China?

Australia has no general prohibition on importing coal-fired generation equipment, but practical demand is minimal. Most Chinese manufacturers of coal equipment have pivoted to renewable and grid equipment production. If your business requires components for existing coal infrastructure (for maintenance or upgrade purposes), the supply chain is narrower but still functional.

### What certifications do Chinese solar modules need for Australian installation?

Chinese solar modules must be tested and certified to IEC standards (IEC 61215 for crystalline silicon, IEC 61646 for thin film) and must be listed on the Clean Energy Regulator's (CER) list of approved equipment to qualify for STCs (Small-scale Technology Certificates). Inverters must comply with AS/NZS 4777.2:2015 and be CEC-approved. Always verify current certification status on the CER website before purchasing.

### Is Chinese battery equipment safe for Australian homes and businesses?

Chinese-manufactured LFP (lithium iron phosphate) battery cells are widely regarded as safe and are used in utility-scale installations globally. The safety risk profile depends more on the integration, installation quality, and battery management system (BMS) than on the cell manufacturer. Ensure your installer is CEC-accredited and that the system complies with AS/NZS 5139.

### How do I find a reliable Chinese energy equipment supplier?

Approaches include attending trade shows such as the China International Trade Fair for Renewable Energy (CIFF) or Intersolar Europe (where many Chinese manufacturers exhibit), engaging a sourcing agent with energy sector experience, and requesting references from previous Australian buyers. Always conduct factory audits for orders above AUD 100,000 — this is standard practice in the industry.

## The Broader Energy Transition and Your Supply Chain

The Liddell closure is not an isolated event — it is one node in a broader transformation of the global energy system. Australian businesses that are importing from China, or that are considering doing so for energy equipment, should recognise that they are operating in a sellers' market for many product categories. Lead times are extended, pricing is firm, and the gap between top-tier and second-tier Chinese manufacturers has widened.

The businesses that will navigate this transition most effectively are those that:

1. Plan procurement well ahead of actual need
2. Build relationships with established manufacturers rather than chasing the lowest price
3. Verify certifications and compliance requirements before ordering
4. Engage local expertise for installation and grid connection
5. Consider long-term service and warranty support as part of the total cost of ownership

The energy transition will continue to generate significant demand for equipment sourced from Chinese manufacturers. The Liddell closure is, in this sense, a preview of what the next decade will look like for Australia's energy supply chain.
